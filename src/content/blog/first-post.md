A few months ago, while scrolling through Pinterest, I started wondering if I could recreate some of the graphic designs I saw as posters. My first piece began with an image of a Greek statue. I opened it in Photoshop and started experimenting without a clear end goal. I tend to let an idea lead me, then decide when a piece feels finished. It may seem a little strange, but it works for me.

While I was working, a phrase came to mind: **“Physical media, the way to go.”** I had recently been watching a YouTuber talk about the right to repair, and there was a discussion at the time about games no longer being released on physical discs. That thought found its way into the first piece—and the quote became a detail I’ve carried into every artwork since.

I tried making different dithered versions of the image, including CMYK, Game Boy, and Apple II styles. Then I layered them in Photoshop, changed their opacity, and rearranged which versions sat on top. Somehow, it all came together. I was genuinely surprised by how easy the process felt and how professional the result looked. It was surreal to make something I was proud of almost by accident.

Since then, digital art has become a small hobby. When something is bothering me, or an idea keeps nagging at me, I can work it out in an image. I often find myself returning to Greek statues and Latin phrases. I’ve always been drawn to Latin quotes, and I’ve also had a lasting connection to language thanks to my sixth-year Irish teacher, Mr. Phelan. Before his class, I felt much like everyone else: bored and unmotivated. Over ten weeks, he helped me see Irish differently and made it fun and interesting. That experience still shapes the way I think about expressing myself.

I don’t always know what I’m trying to say when I start making a piece. Sometimes I’ll find an image on Pinterest, take it in a direction of my own, and add a quote that feels right. The process is part of the point: follow the idea, experiment, and stop when it feels done.
